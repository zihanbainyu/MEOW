# Neural mechanisms of interaction between episodic memory and working memory

**Request for additional funding for an ongoing pilot study**

Applicants: Zihan Bai (NYU PhD in C&P, global program) and Sebastian Michelmann (NYU).

**Introduction:** Working memory (WM) maintains information after it leaves the sensory environment, but its capacity is limited (Luck & Vogel, 1997). Episodic memory (EM) rapidly encodes distinct, high-resolution representations of individual events (Tulving, 1983). Our pilot study set out to localize the site of EM-WM integration and to assess the neural mechanisms that endow EM with its ability to support WM. With the initial award we scanned nine participants in the MST-back task while recording eye movements. **These data confirm our primary hypothesis and identify the reinstatement of episodic content in cortex as a mechanism by which EM supports WM. We request additional scanner time to double the sample (N = 18) and to add the high-resolution hippocampal imaging that is required to test the remaining hypotheses at the level of hippocampal subfields.**

**Progress report:**

**Data collection.** All tokens of the initial award have been used. Nine participants completed the MST-back protocol in the scanner: four pairs of one-back (encoding) and two-back (retrieval) runs, followed by a final mnemonic similarity run, with concurrent eye tracking. Data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep, and single-trial responses were estimated for every image presentation (920 MST-back trials per participant).

**Behavior.** We replicated our previous behavioral finding inside the scanner: in the two-back phase, similar pair-mates that had previously been compared to each other were discriminated better than novel pair-mates, while the detection of identical items showed no evidence of modulation by prior experience.

**Primary hypothesis (univariate).** We predicted that a shared subnetwork modulates the successful discrimination of similar pair-mates when EM is available. We tested this prediction in an a priori network of EM regions by contrasting the BOLD response for successful vs. unsuccessful discrimination between the compared condition (EM available) and the novel condition (no EM available). **The anterior hippocampus was more active for successful than unsuccessful discrimination of compared pair-mates (p < .001), but not of novel pair-mates (condition × accuracy interaction: p = .012).** Perirhinal cortex showed the same success effect for compared pair-mates (p = .040), and the angular gyrus showed an effect in the same direction (p = .051). This success signal was specific in two ways. First, none of these regions distinguished successful from unsuccessful detection of identical items. Second, object-selective visual regions (ventral temporal and lateral occipital cortex) tracked successful discrimination in both the compared and the novel condition, i.e., regardless of whether an episodic memory was available. In an independent contrast from the final mnemonic similarity run, memory success was likewise predicted by the posterior hippocampus (p = .022), the angular gyrus (p = .007), and the precuneus (p = .011), confirming that the regions that drive these effects belong to the EM network.

**Primary hypothesis (multivariate).** We first localized regions that track image similarity: a whole-brain searchlight showed that the similarity of voxel patterns follows the similarity of the images in early and ventral visual cortex and in lateral occipital cortex (N = 9, TFCE, FWE-corrected p < .05). We then tested how EM supports WM. Following our proposal, we compared the voxel pattern during target presentation in the two-back phase with the voxel pattern of the same target in the one-back phase (B2-B1 similarity; pattern completion). **Pattern completion was higher for successful than for unsuccessful discrimination in ventral temporal cortex (p = .034) and lateral occipital cortex (p = .026)**, with promising effects in the same direction in perirhinal cortex (p = .079) and the angular gyrus (p = .057), and no evidence for such an effect in early visual cortex or in the anterior hippocampus. The hippocampus thus signals successful discrimination, while the content of the episodic memory is reinstated in cortex. Consistent with a coordination between the two, trial-wise reinstatement in the anterior hippocampus was coupled with reinstatement in the angular gyrus (p = .003), perirhinal cortex (p = .013), ventral temporal cortex (p < .001), and lateral occipital cortex (p = .009), but not in a ventromedial prefrontal control region; in the angular gyrus, this coupling tended to be stronger for successful discrimination (p = .052). Signatures of pattern separation (A1-B1 similarity) and predictive recall (A2-B1 similarity) were not yet detectable in the pilot sample. In our behavioral study these were the smaller effects, and pattern separation is predicted specifically in the dentate gyrus, which the pilot data cannot resolve (see below).

**Update on design:**

**The MST-back task in the scanner.** We streamlined the task for fMRI. Each run contrasts previously compared pair-mates with novel pair-mates; the isolated condition was dropped in order to maximize the number of trials in the two conditions that define our primary hypothesis. Each one-back run comprises 130 trials and each two-back run 100 trials, with a slower event timing than in the behavioral study (1.5 s image presentation, mean SOA ≈ 3 s) that supports the estimation of single-trial responses. Across the four two-back runs, every participant contributes 40 similar-item discriminations in the compared and 40 in the novel condition. The final mnemonic similarity run comprises 120 trials.

**Analysis.** Because every image is presented only once per task phase, single-trial responses are estimated with a least-squares-separate model. Pattern similarity is expressed as the rank correlation between single-trial voxel patterns relative to a baseline of non-matching items, which directly mirrors the analysis of gaze-pattern reinstatement in our previous study. All hypotheses are tested in a network of EM regions that was specified a priori (hippocampus, perirhinal cortex, angular gyrus, ventral temporal and lateral occipital cortex), with early visual cortex and prefrontal regions as controls.

**Planned extension.** (1) We will scan nine additional participants with the identical functional protocol, for a combined sample of N = 18. The pattern completion effects in the pilot sample correspond to effect sizes of d ≈ 0.6-0.75; at N = 18, the power to detect an effect of d = 0.6 increases from 50% to 79%. (2) Hippocampal analyses to date rely on an anterior/posterior division of the hippocampus, because the first nine sessions do not include a T2 image with sufficient in-plane resolution for subfield segmentation. Every additional session will therefore include the high-resolution oblique-coronal T2 (0.4 × 0.4 × 2 mm, spanning the hippocampal long axis) for automated subfield segmentation (ASHS-T2; DG/CA3, CA1, subiculum). This allows us to test our remaining predictions where we expect them: pattern separation in the dentate gyrus and pattern completion in CA3. (3) Eye tracking in the scanner yielded usable data in seven of nine participants; the gaze-pattern effects from our behavioral study are in the predicted direction in this subsample. With the combined sample we will relate trial-wise gaze reinstatement to neural reinstatement.

The behavioral study that motivates this work has been submitted for publication.
